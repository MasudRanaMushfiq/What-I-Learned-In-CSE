## Chapter 1
## Foundations of Engineering Economy

### 1.1 What is Engineering Economics?

**Engineering Economics** applies economic principles to evaluate the costs, benefits, and risks of engineering projects so that engineers can make rational financial decisions.

**Role in Decision Making:**
- Provides a systematic framework to compare engineering alternatives.
- Helps justify expenditures and identify the most cost-effective solution.
- Bridges the gap between technical and financial decision-making.

### 1.1.1 Importance and Role of Engineering Economics

Engineering economics is vital for translating technical designs into financially viable projects. Its importance includes:

Informed Decision-Making: It quantifies costs, benefits, and risks, allowing for objective choices between alternatives.

Resource Optimization: It ensures the efficient use of materials, labor, and funds by considering the time value of money.

Project Viability and Planning: It determines if a project will yield a positive return on investment (ROI) and assists in creating realistic budgets from the outset.

Risk Mitigation: It identifies potential pitfalls and helps develop strategies to manage uncertainties.

### 1.2 Interest Rate and Rate of Return

- **Interest:** The cost of borrowing money, or the return earned on an investment.
- **Interest Rate ($i$):** Interest per unit time expressed as a percentage of the principal.
- **Rate of Return (ROR):** The interest rate earned on the unrecovered balance of an investment over its life.

**Types of Interest:**

| Type | Description | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple Interest** | Calculated on the **principal only** | $I = P \times i \times n$ |
| **Compound Interest** | Calculated on principal **plus accumulated interest** ("interest on interest") | $F = P(1+i)^n$ |

> 📌 **Compound interest** is the standard assumption in all engineering economy calculations unless stated otherwise.

### 1.3 Terminology and Symbols

Five standard symbols are used in all engineering economy formulas:

| Symbol | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $P$ | Present Value | A sum of money at the **present** time (time = 0) |
| $F$ | Future Value | A sum of money at some **future** point in time |
| $A$ | Annual / Uniform Series | An **equal** end-of-period cash flow each period |
| $n$ | Number of Periods | The total number of interest periods (years, months, etc.) |
| $i$ | Interest Rate | The effective interest rate **per period** |

### 1.4 Cash Flows: Estimation and Diagramming

- **Cash Flow:** The actual timing and magnitude of money flowing **in** (receipts) or **out** (disbursements).
- **Cash Flow Diagram:** A timeline where upward arrows represent **receipts** (+) and downward arrows represent **disbursements** (−).

**Categories of Cash Flows:**

| Category | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **First Cost** | Initial investment at time 0 | Purchasing equipment |
| **Operating Costs** | Recurring annual expenses | Maintenance, fuel, salaries |
| **Salvage Value** | Residual value at end of life | Resale of machinery |
| **Revenue / Benefits** | Positive returns from the investment | Product sales, cost savings |

> 💡 Always draw a cash flow diagram before solving any problem — it clarifies the perspective (borrower vs. lender) and the timing of each cash flow.

### 1.5 Professional Ethics and Economic Decisions

- Engineers must maintain **honesty and integrity** in all economic estimates.
- **Biased Estimation** — deliberately skewing cost/benefit figures to favor a preferred alternative — is an ethical violation.
- The **NSPE Code of Ethics** requires engineers to act as faithful agents for clients and employers, always disclosing conflicts of interest.

### 1.5.1 Morals vs. Ethics in Economics

While often used interchangeably, the source distinguishes them within an economic context:

Ethics: The application of moral principles to guide decision-making toward socially responsible and sustainable systems, ensuring fairness and environmental protection.

Morals: The underlying values that ensure economic activities align with social well-being and responsibility toward communities and the environment.

### 1.6 Detailed Categorization of Services and Products

The sources provide specific classifications for outputs that satisfy human needs:

#### Types of Services:

Healthcare: Medical treatments and consultations.
Educational: Teaching and training platforms.
Financial: Banking, insurance, and wealth management.
Transportation: Logistics and moving people/goods.
Utilities: Essential services like water, electricity, and sanitation.

#### Types of Products:

Durable vs. Non-Durable: Durable goods have a long lifespan (appliances, vehicles), while non-durable goods are consumed quickly (food, toiletries).
Intermediate Goods: Materials used in production rather than for end-consumption.
Capital Goods: Tools and machinery used by businesses for operations.
Commodities: Standardized raw materials like gold, oil, or agricultural products.

### 1.7 Mathematical Market Modeling

Market behavior is analyzed using specific demand and supply equations:

**Demand Equation (Q_D = a - bP):** Where a is the intercept (quantity at price 0) and b is the slope representing the change in quantity per unit price change.
**Supply Equation (Q_S = c + dP):** Where c is the intercept and d is the slope showing how quantity supplied responds to price.
**Equilibrium Calculation:** Determined by setting Q_D = Q_S.

Example: If Q_D = 17 - 3P and Q_S = 3 + 4P, then 17 - 3P = 3 + 4P results in an equilibrium price of P = 2 and quantity Q = 11.

### 1.8 Advanced Elasticity Measures

Beyond simple price elasticity, several specialized measures determine how demand reacts to various factors:

**Arc Elasticity:** Calculates elasticity over a range or interval of prices rather than a single point, useful for discrete data.

**Formula:** PED = ( (Q_2 - Q_1) / ((Q_1 + Q_2)/2) ) / ( (P_2 - P_1) / ((P_1 + P_2)/2) )

**Cross-Price Elasticity (XED):** Measures responsiveness of Good A's demand to price changes in Good B.

Substitutes (XED > 0): Price of one increases, demand for the other increases.

Complements (XED < 0): Price of one increases, demand for the other decreases.

Income Elasticity (YED): Measures sensitivity to consumer income changes.

Luxury Good (YED > 1): Demand increases proportionally more than income.

Necessity (0 < YED < 1): Demand increases proportionally less than income.

Inferior Good (YED < 0): Demand decreases as income increases.

### 1.9 National Income and Inflation Metrics

**National Income Formulas:**

Real GDP Growth Rate: (Current Year GDP - Previous Year GDP) / Previous Year GDP × 100

Per Capita GDP: Total GDP / Total Population

**Measures of Inflation:**

Consumer Price Index (CPI): Tracks a fixed basket of goods for urban households.

Producer Price Index (PPI): Tracks price changes at the production level.

GDP Deflator: A broad indicator covering all goods and services in the GDP.

Causes of Inflation: These include Demand-Pull (demand exceeds supply), Cost-Push (rising production costs), and Built-In (wage-price cycles).

### 1.10 Theory of Cost

Analyzing production requires understanding different cost structures:

Fixed Costs (FC): Expenses that remain constant regardless of production levels (e.g., rent, salaries).

Variable Costs (VC): Costs that change directly with output (e.g., raw materials).

Total Cost (TC): The sum of fixed and variable costs (TC = FC + VC).

Marginal Cost (MC): The cost of producing one additional unit of output.

Average Cost (AC): The total cost divided by the quantity produced (AC = TC/Q).

### 1.11 Taxation Systems

Governments impose taxes to generate revenue for public services:

Direct Taxes: Levied directly on income, profits, or assets (e.g., Income Tax, Corporate Tax).

Indirect Taxes: Levied on the consumption of goods and services (e.g., Sales Tax, Excise Tax).

Value-Added Tax (VAT): An indirect tax applied at each stage of production or distribution based on the value added at that step.

## Chapter 2
## Engineering Economy Factors and Formulas

### 2.1 Single-Amount Factors

Used when a single lump sum is moved between the present and a future point.

| Factor | Notation | Formula | Finds | Given |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Compound Amount** | $(F/P,\, i,\, n)$ | $F = P(1+i)^n$ | $F$ | $P$ |
| **Present Worth** | $(P/F,\, i,\, n)$ | $P = F(1+i)^{-n}$ | $P$ | $F$ |

> 📌 The factor notation $(X/Y,\, i,\, n)$ means: **"Find $X$, Given $Y$"** at interest rate $i$ over $n$ periods.

### 2.2 Uniform Series Factors

Used when equal payments $A$ occur at the **end of each period**.

| Factor | Notation | Finds | Given | Common Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Present Worth** | $(P/A,\, i,\, n)$ | $P$ | $A$ | PW of an annuity |
| **Capital Recovery** | $(A/P,\, i,\, n)$ | $A$ | $P$ | Annual loan payment |
| **Compound Amount** | $(F/A,\, i,\, n)$ | $F$ | $A$ | Future value of savings |
| **Sinking Fund** | $(A/F,\, i,\, n)$ | $A$ | $F$ | Annual deposit to reach a target $F$ |

**Key Formula — Capital Recovery:**

$$A = P \left[\frac{i(1+i)^n}{(1+i)^n - 1}\right]$$

### 2.3 Gradient Factors

Used when cash flows **change systematically** each period.

#### Arithmetic Gradient ($G$)
Cash flows increase or decrease by a **constant amount** $G$ each period:

$$P_G = G \left[\frac{(1+i)^n - in - 1}{i^2(1+i)^n}\right]$$

- The **total present worth** combines a base annuity ($A$) with the gradient component ($G$).

#### Geometric Gradient ($g$)
Cash flows change by a **constant percentage** $g$ each period:

$$P = \frac{A_1}{i - g}\left[1 - \left(\frac{1+g}{1+i}\right)^n\right] \quad \text{for } i \neq g$$

> 💡 Geometric gradients are useful for modeling **inflation-escalated costs** or **growing revenues** (e.g., energy costs rising 5% per year).

### 2.4 Determining Unknown $i$ or $n$

When $P$, $F$, and $A$ are all known, the interest rate $i$ or number of periods $n$ can be found by:
- **Interpolation** from standard factor tables.
- Direct solution using a **financial calculator or spreadsheet** (e.g., Excel `RATE()` or `NPER()` functions).

## Chapter 3
## Nominal and Effective Interest Rates

### 3.1 Nominal vs. Effective Interest Rate

| Rate Type | Symbol | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominal Rate** | $r$ | Stated annual rate; does **not** account for compounding within the year |
| **Effective Annual Rate** | $i_a$ | Actual annual rate after accounting for intra-year compounding |
| **Effective Rate per Period** | $i$ | Rate actually applied each compounding period |

**Conversion Formula:**

$$i_a = \left(1 + \frac{r}{m}\right)^m - 1$$

where $m$ = number of compounding periods per year.

> 📌 Example: A nominal rate of **12% compounded monthly** gives an effective annual rate of $(1 + 0.01)^{12} - 1 = \mathbf{12.68\%}$.

### 3.2 Effective Interest Rate for Any Time Period

If the compounding period and payment period differ, find the **effective rate matching the payment period**:

$$i_{\text{period}} = \left(1 + \frac{r}{m}\right)^{m/\text{payments per year}} - 1$$

### 3.3 Continuous Compounding

When compounding occurs **infinitely often** per year, the effective annual rate becomes:

$$i_a = e^r - 1$$

where $e \approx 2.71828$ is Euler's number and $r$ is the nominal rate.

| Compounding Frequency | Effective Annual Rate (at $r = 12\%$) |
|---|---|
| Annually ($m=1$) | 12.000% |
| Monthly ($m=12$) | 12.683% |
| Daily ($m=365$) | 12.747% |
| Continuously ($m \to \infty$) | 12.750% |

### 3.4 Interest Rates That Vary Over Time

When the interest rate changes each period, the **present worth** is found by chaining discount factors:

$$P = F \cdot \frac{1}{(1+i_1)(1+i_2)\cdots(1+i_n)}$$

> 💡 Variable interest rates commonly arise in **adjustable-rate loans** or long-term project analyses spanning changing economic conditions.

## Chapter 4
## Benefit/Cost Analysis and Public Sector Economics

### 4.1 Public Sector vs. Private Sector

| Attribute | Public Sector | Private Sector |
|---|---|---|
| **Objective** | Maximize social benefit | Maximize profit |
| **Funding** | Taxes, bonds, user fees | Equity, debt, retained earnings |
| **Interest Rate** | Low (social discount rate) | Higher (MARR-based) |
| **Project Life** | Very long (30–100 years) | Shorter (5–20 years) |
| **Examples** | Dams, highways, hospitals | Factories, software, retail |

### 4.2 Benefit/Cost Analysis of a Single Project

The **B/C ratio** compares the present worth of benefits to the present worth of costs:

$$B/C = \frac{\text{PW of Benefits}}{\text{PW of Costs}} = \frac{B}{C}$$

**Decision Rule:**

| B/C Value | Decision |
|---|---|
| $B/C \geq 1.0$ | **Economically justified** — benefits outweigh costs |
| $B/C < 1.0$ | **Not justified** — costs exceed benefits |

> 📌 **Disbenefits** (negative consequences to the public) are subtracted from benefits, not added to costs: $B/C = (B - D)/C$.

### 4.3 Incremental B/C Analysis for Multiple Alternatives

When comparing **mutually exclusive alternatives**, use **incremental analysis** — evaluate the additional benefits and costs of moving from one alternative to the next:

$$\Delta B/C = \frac{\Delta B}{\Delta C}$$

- Order alternatives by **increasing total cost**.
- If $\Delta B/C \geq 1.0$, the **higher-cost alternative is preferred**.
- Continue comparing until all alternatives are evaluated.

### 4.4 Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (Service Sector)

When benefits **cannot be expressed in monetary terms** (e.g., lives saved, students educated), use the **Cost-Effectiveness (C/E) ratio**:

$$C/E = \frac{\text{Total Cost}}{\text{Effectiveness Measure}}$$

The alternative with the **lowest C/E ratio** delivers the best value per unit of effectiveness.

## Chapter 5
## Effects of Inflation

### 5.1 Understanding Inflation

- **Inflation:** A general rise in price levels over time, reducing the **purchasing power** of money.
- **Inflation Rate ($f$):** The annual percentage increase in the general price level.

**Two Types of Currency:**

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Actual (Current) Dollars** | Dollars with the purchasing power of the **year in which they occur** |
| **Constant (Real) Dollars** | Dollars with the purchasing power of a **fixed base year** |

**Conversion:**

$$\text{Constant \$} = \frac{\text{Actual \$}}{(1+f)^n}$$

### 5.2 Present Worth Adjusted for Inflation

The **inflation-adjusted interest rate** ($i_f$) combines the real rate ($i$) and inflation rate ($f$):

$$i_f = i + f + (i \times f)$$

**Adjusted PW Formula (using Actual Dollars):**

$$P = F \cdot (P/F,\, i_f,\, n)$$

> 📌 When **ignoring** inflation (using constant dollars), apply the **real rate** $i$. When **including** inflation (using actual dollars), apply the **inflation-adjusted rate** $i_f$.

### 5.3 Future Worth and Capital Recovery Adjusted for Inflation

- **Future Worth:** Compute $F$ in actual dollars using $i_f$ as the interest rate.
- **Capital Recovery:** The annual payment increases when inflation is accounted for, since the purchasing power of future payments is lower.

$$A_{\text{actual}} = P \cdot (A/P,\, i_f,\, n)$$

> 💡 Inflation **always increases** the required annual payment compared to the non-inflation case, because the same nominal dollars are worth less each year.

## Chapter 6
## After-Tax Economic Analysis

### 6.1 Income Tax Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Gross Income (GI)** | Total revenue before any deductions |
| **Operating Expenses (OE)** | Allowable business costs deducted from GI |
| **Depreciation ($D$)** | Non-cash deduction for asset wear over its life |
| **Taxable Income (TI)** | Income subject to tax |
| **Taxes ($T$)** | Amount paid to the government: $T = TI \times \text{tax rate}$ |
| **Cash Flow After Tax (CFAT)** | Net cash flow remaining after taxes |

**Core Relation:**

$$TI = \text{Gross Revenue} - \text{Operating Expenses} - \text{Depreciation}$$

$$CFAT = \text{Cash Flow Before Tax} - \text{Taxes}$$

### 6.2 Depreciation Methods and Their Tax Effect

| Method | Description | Tax Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Straight Line (SL)** | Equal depreciation each year: $D = \frac{B - S_v}{n}$ | Lower deductions early, higher taxes early |
| **MACRS** | IRS-mandated; accelerated, zero salvage value assumed | **Larger deductions early** → lower early taxes → better PW |
| **Declining Balance (DB)** | Fixed percentage of book value each year | Front-loaded deductions |

> 📌 **MACRS** (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System) is the standard tax depreciation method in the U.S. It provides **larger deductions in earlier years**, improving after-tax cash flows and present worth.

### 6.3 Depreciation Recapture and Capital Gains

| Event | Occurs When | Tax Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Depreciation Recapture** | Asset sold for more than **book value** | Recaptured amount taxed as **ordinary income** |
| **Capital Gain** | Asset sold for more than **first cost** | Gain taxed at **capital gains rate** |
| **Capital Loss** | Asset sold for less than **book value** | Loss provides a **tax savings** |

### 6.4 After-Tax Replacement Study

- Compare the **Defender** (existing asset) against the **Challenger** (new asset) using **after-tax Annual Worth**.
- Include the **market value of the defender** as an opportunity cost (foregone if kept).
- Depreciation and tax effects differ for defender (partially depreciated) vs. challenger (new MACRS schedule).

### 6.5 Value-Added Tax (VAT)

- **VAT** is a consumption tax levied at **each stage of the supply chain** on the value added to a product.
- Common in international projects outside the U.S.
- For engineering economy, VAT increases project costs and must be included in the cash flow analysis for **international projects**.
