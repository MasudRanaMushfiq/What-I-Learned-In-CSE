## Chapter 1
## Course Overview and Human Values

### 1.1 Course Overview
The primary objectives of this course are to **understand the moral values** that guide the engineering profession, **resolve moral issues** encountered in practice, and **justify moral judgments**. It aims to develop the intellect through cognitive skills such as moral awareness, reasoning, and imagination, while fostering a commitment to responsible conduct. The study of professional ethics is essential in today's environment of professional conflict and stress to refine behavior and decision-making for the benefit of the family, organization, and society.

### 1.2 Morals, Values, and Ethics

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Morals** | Welfare principles enunciated by wise people based on experience and wisdom; they relate to what "ought" or "ought not" to be done in a situation. |
| **Values** | Guidelines for success or principles that promote well-being and prevent harm. Core values include right conduct, peace, truth, love, and non-violence. |
| **Ethics** | The study of morals, values, and beliefs of individuals or society. |

### 1.3 Integrity and Virtues
- **Integrity** is defined as the unity of thought, word, and deed, characterized by honesty and open-mindedness.
- **Virtues** are positive, preferred values and desirable character traits (e.g., honesty, courage, fairness) that enable individuals to reach their potential. **Civic virtues** involve the moral duties and rights of a citizen, such as paying taxes, following safety rules, and protecting the environment.

### 1.4 Fundamental Human Values

> 📌 The following values constitute the foundation of ethical human conduct:

| Value Category | Specific Values |
| --- | --- |
| **Core Values** | Right conduct, peace, truth, love, and non-violence |
| **Interpersonal Values** | Respect for others, Living peacefully, Caring, Sharing, Honesty, Cooperation, Empathy |
| **Personal Virtues** | Courage, Valuing time, Commitment, Self-confidence, Character, Spirituality |
| **Wellness Practices** | Introduction to Yoga and meditation |

### 1.5 Work Ethics
**Work Ethics:** A set of attitudes concerned with the value of work. It emphasizes that work is intrinsically valuable, promotes self-respect, and fulfills obligations to society.

### 1.6 Service Learning
**Service Learning:** A methodology of experiential education where service is provided on a voluntary basis to the community while integrated into a curriculum.

## Chapter 2
## Work Ethics and Moral Development

### 2.1 Senses of Engineering Ethics

| Sense | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Normative sense** | Knowing moral values and finding accurate solutions to moral problems. |
| **Descriptive sense** | Referring to what specific groups of engineers believe and how they act. |

### 2.2 Types of Inquiries

| Type | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| **Normative** | Identifying and justifying standards to guide individuals. |
| **Conceptual** | Clarifying the meaning of concepts (e.g., "What is safety?"). |
| **Factual/Descriptive** | Obtaining facts needed to resolve value issues. |

### 2.3 Variety of Moral Issues
Engineering practice involves a wide range of moral issues, including:
- Honesty in data representation
- Fairness in resource allocation
- Transparency in reporting
- Accountability for failures
- Respect for intellectual property
- Balancing competing stakeholder interests

### 2.4 Moral Dilemmas and Autonomy
- **Moral Dilemmas** arise when moral reasons conflict, or when application of values is unclear due to vagueness, conflicting reasons, or disagreement.
- **Moral Autonomy** is the skill of thinking rationally about ethical issues based on moral concern, rather than passive adoption of social conventions.

> 💡 **Moral Autonomy** is defined as independence in making decisions based on critical moral reflection.

### 2.5 Moral Development Theories

#### Kohlberg's Theory
Proposes three levels of moral development:

| Level | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| **Pre-conventional** | Self-interest / avoiding punishment |
| **Conventional** | Respecting law and loyalty |
| **Post-conventional** | Universal principles and moral integrity |

#### Gilligan's Theory
Criticized Kohlberg's male bias; emphasized an **"ethics of care"** and maintaining personal relationships rather than just rigid rules.

### 2.6 Consensus, Controversy, and Professional Roles

| Concept | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Consensus and Controversy** | The dynamic interplay between shared moral agreements and ongoing disagreements in professional ethics. |
| **Models of professional roles** | Different frameworks for understanding the engineer's responsibilities (e.g., hired gun, guardian, social servant). |
| **Theories about right action** | Philosophical frameworks (utilitarianism, duty ethics, virtue ethics) that guide ethical decision-making. |
| **Self-interest** | The legitimate consideration of one's own welfare, which must be balanced against professional obligations. |
| **Customs and Religion** | Traditional and faith-based sources of moral guidance that influence ethical perspectives. |
| **Ethical Theories** | Systematic approaches to understanding and justifying moral principles. |

## Chapter 3
## Engineering as Social Experimentation

### 3.1 The Experimental Nature of Engineering
Engineering projects should be viewed as experiments because they involve partial ignorance, uncertain outcomes, and require continuous monitoring.

### 3.2 Informed Consent
**Informed Consent:** Subjects (the public/users) must be given relevant information to make a voluntary decision to participate or use a product.

### 3.3 Codes of Ethics
These provide inspiration, guidance, and support for engineers, while serving as a deterrent against unethical behavior. Limitations include vague wordings and potential internal conflicts.

## Chapter 4
## Safety, Responsibilities, and Rights

### 4.1 Safety and Risk

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Safety** | Risk that is known and judged as acceptable. |
| **Risk** | The potential for something unwanted and harmful to occur. |

### 4.2 Assessment of Safety and Risk

**Risk Analysis Tools:**

| Tool | Full Name | Methodology |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **FTA** | Fault-Tree Analysis | A deductive logic method that starts with an undesirable event and traces it back to basic causes. |
| **ETA** | Event-Tree Analysis | An inductive logic method that explores outcomes following a selected initial event. |

**Risk Benefit Analysis:** A systematic evaluation of the potential harms (risks) and advantages (benefits) associated with a technology, project, or decision to determine if it is acceptable.

### 4.3 Professional Responsibilities

| Responsibility Area | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Collegiality** | Supporting colleagues in their professional roles. |
| **Loyalty** | Distinguished as agency loyalty (faithful service) vs. attitude loyalty (identification with employer's interests). |
| **Conflicts of Interest** | Situations that can be actual, apparent, or potential where personal interests interfere with professional obligations. |
| **Confidentiality** | The obligation to protect sensitive information obtained in a professional capacity. |
| **Respect for Authority** | Acknowledging legitimate organizational hierarchies while maintaining ethical independence. |
| **Collective Bargaining** | Negotiations between employer and employee representatives regarding working conditions and compensation. |
| **Occupational Crime** | Illegal acts committed during the course of employment, often for personal or organizational gain. |
| **Discrimination** | Unfair treatment of individuals based on protected characteristics such as race, gender, religion, or age. |

### 4.4 Rights

| Category | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Professional Rights** | Rights held by professionals in their capacity as experts (e.g., right of professional conscience). |
| **Employee Rights** | Rights of workers in employment contexts (e.g., privacy, equal opportunity, fair treatment). |
| **Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)** | Legal rights protecting creations of the mind. |

### 4.5 Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)

| IPR Type | Duration | Subject Matter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Patents** | 20 years | New products, processes, or inventions |
| **Copyrights** | Life + 70 years (typically) | Literary, artistic, and musical works |
| **Trademarks** | Renewable every 10 years | Symbols, names, logos identifying goods |
| **Trade Secrets** | Indefinite (as long as kept secret) | Confidential business information |

## Chapter 5
## Global Issues and Professional Practice

### 5.1 Multinational Corporations (MNCs)
MNCs must respect the basic human rights and laws of host countries while providing fair remuneration and ensuring safety standards.

### 5.2 Environmental Ethics
**Environmental Ethics:** The study of moral issues concerning the environment. Engineers must strive for **sustainable development**—meeting present needs without compromising future generations.

### 5.3 Computer Ethics
Deals with issues like **hacking**, **spreading viruses**, and **privacy** violations.

### 5.4 Weapons Development
A controversial area of engineering ethics that raises questions about the moral responsibility of engineers who design and develop weapon systems, including considerations of proportionality, discrimination, and the potential for misuse.

### 5.5 Engineers in Various Roles

| Role | Key Responsibilities |
| --- | --- |
| **Engineers as Managers** | Balancing technical oversight with ethical leadership, employee welfare, and organizational accountability. |
| **Consulting Engineers** | Providing expert advice independently while maintaining objectivity and avoiding conflicts of interest. |
| **Engineers as Expert Witnesses and Advisors** | Testifying honestly in legal proceedings and advising decision-makers with professional integrity. |

### 5.6 Moral Leadership and Corporate Ethics

| Concept | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Moral Leadership** | Leading by example, demonstrating ethical conduct, and inspiring others to act with integrity. |
| **Code of Conduct** | A formal document stating an organization's ethical principles and expected behaviors. |
| **Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)** | A self-regulating business model that helps a company be socially accountable to itself, its stakeholders, and the public. |

## Chapter 6
## Technology, Society, and Environment

### 6.1 Impact of Technology on Society

| Area | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Innovation and Creativity** | The driving forces behind technological advancement, requiring both technical skill and imaginative thinking. |
| **The history and the trend of technology on the social and culture** | The study of how technological developments have historically shaped — and continue to shape — social structures, cultural practices, and human relationships. |

### 6.2 Environmental Protection and Related Issues
Engineers have a fundamental responsibility to protect the environment through:
- Minimizing pollution and waste
- Using resources efficiently
- Designing for environmental compatibility
- Considering the full lifecycle of products
- Preventing environmental disasters

### 6.3 Role of the Engineer in Energy Conservation, Ecological Balance, and Sustainable Development

> 💡 **Sustainable Development:** Development that meets current needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs.

**The engineer's role includes:**

| Responsibility | Action |
| --- | --- |
| **Energy Conservation** | Designing energy-efficient systems, promoting renewable energy, and reducing waste in processes. |
| **Ecological Balance** | Ensuring projects do not disrupt ecosystems, protecting biodiversity, and remediating environmental damage. |
| **Sustainable Development** | Implementing solutions that balance economic, social, and environmental needs for long-term viability. |

**Additional Key Concepts:**

| Concept | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Cradle-to-Cradle Thinking** | Also known as **biomimicry**, focusing on creating products that can be fully recycled or returned to nature. |
| **Whistle Blowing** | Conveying information about a significant moral problem to someone in a position to take action, often outside approved channels. |

## Important Concepts & Definitions

- **Moral Autonomy:** Independence in making decisions based on critical moral reflection.
- **Whistle Blowing:** Conveying information about a significant moral problem to someone in a position to take action, often outside approved channels.
- **Sustainable Development:** Development that meets current needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- **Cradle-to-Cradle Thinking:** Also known as **biomimicry**, focusing on creating products that can be fully recycled or returned to nature.
- **Conflict of Interest:** A situation where a professional has an interest that might prevent them from meeting obligations to their employer or client.

## Key Examples

| Example | Ethical Lesson |
| --- | --- |
| **Heinz's Dilemma** | A man steals expensive medicine to save his dying wife, used to illustrate differences in moral reasoning between Kohlberg's and Gilligan's theories. |
| **The Challenger Disaster** | A space shuttle tragedy caused by O-ring failure in freezing temperatures. It serves as a lesson in **negligence in design**, the need for **informed consent**, and the dangers of **normalizing deviances**. |
| **Bhopal Gas Tragedy** | A chemical disaster caused by neglected maintenance, lack of training, and the failure of MNCs to implement home-country safety standards in a host country. |
| **Three-Mile Island and Chernobyl** | Nuclear accidents highlighting the need for better containment, continuous monitoring, and learning from past failures. |
