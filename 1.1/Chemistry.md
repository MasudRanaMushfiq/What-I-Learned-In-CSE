## Chapter 1
## Atomic Structure and Periodic Properties

### 1.1 Fundamental Concepts

**Modern Atomic Theory:** Atoms consist of protons, neutrons, and electrons, with electrons organized into specific energy levels or orbitals.

**Quantum Principles:**

| Principle | Statement |
|---|---|
| **Aufbau Principle** | Electrons fill orbitals in order of **increasing energy** |
| **Pauli Exclusion Principle** | No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers; an orbital holds a maximum of **two electrons with opposite spins** |
| **Hund's Rule** | Electrons occupy **separate orbitals** of the same subshell with parallel spins before pairing begins |

---

### 1.2 Periodic Laws and Trends

**Mendeleev vs. Modern Law:**
- **Mendeleev** organized elements by **atomic weight**
- **Modern Periodic Law** defines properties as a periodic function of **atomic number**

**Key Periodic Trends:**

| Property | Across a Period | Down a Group |
|---|---|---|
| **Atomic / Ionic Radius** | Decreases (increasing nuclear charge) | Increases (additional shells) |
| **Ionization Energy** | Increases | Decreases |
| **Electron Affinity** | Generally increases | Decreases |
| **Electronegativity** | Increases | Decreases |

> 📌 **Ionization Energy** is the energy required to remove an electron from a neutral atom.
> **Electron Affinity** is the energy released when an electron is added to a neutral atom.
> **Electronegativity** is the ability of an atom to attract shared electrons in a bond.

---

### 1.3 Nuclear Charge and Shielding

- **Shielding Effect:** Inner electrons reduce the attractive force exerted by the nucleus on valence electrons.
- **Effective Nuclear Charge ($Z^*$):** The net positive charge experienced by a valence electron:

$$Z^* = Z - S$$

where $Z$ is the atomic number and $S$ is the **screening constant** determined by **Slater's Rules**.

## Chapter 2
## Chemical Bonding

### 2.1 The Octet Rule and Stability

Atoms form bonds to achieve a stable noble gas configuration — typically **eight valence electrons**.

**Exceptions:**
- **Incomplete octet** — e.g., $BF_3$ (boron has only 6 electrons)
- **Expanded octet** — e.g., $PCl_5$ (phosphorus exceeds 8 electrons using d-orbitals)

---

### 2.2 Primary Bond Types

| Bond Type | Formation Mechanism | Key Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Ionic Bond** | **Transfer of electrons** between atoms with large electronegativity differences | Non-directional; forms 3D crystal lattices |
| **Covalent Bond** | **Sharing of electrons** between atoms | Directional; forms discrete molecules |
| **Coordinate (Dative) Bond** | Both shared electrons provided by a **single donor atom** | A special case of covalent bonding |
| **Metallic Bond** | **Electron Sea Model** — positive metal ions in a sea of delocalized valence electrons | Explains electrical conductivity and malleability |

**Types of Covalent Bonds:**

| Bond | Formation | Property |
|---|---|---|
| **Sigma ($\sigma$) Bond** | Head-on orbital overlap | Allows **free rotation** along the bond axis |
| **Pi ($\pi$) Bond** | Sideways (lateral) orbital overlap | **Prevents rotation**; found in double and triple bonds |

---

### 2.3 Bonding Theories and Geometry

#### Valence Bond (VB) Theory
- Focuses on the **overlap of individual atomic orbitals** to form bonds.
- Bond strength depends on the degree of overlap.

#### Hybridization
The mixing of atomic orbitals of similar energy to form new **hybrid orbitals** that determine molecular geometry:

| Hybridization | Geometry | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $sp$ | Linear | $BeCl_2$ |
| $sp^2$ | Trigonal planar | $BF_3$ |
| $sp^3$ | Tetrahedral | $CH_4$ |
| $sp^3d$ | Trigonal bipyramidal | $PCl_5$ |
| $sp^3d^2$ | Octahedral | $SF_6$ |

#### VSEPR Theory
- Predicts molecular shapes based on the **repulsion between electron pairs**.
- **Lone pairs repel more strongly** than bond pairs, distorting ideal geometry.

#### Molecular Orbital (MO) Theory
- Atomic orbitals combine to form **bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals** that belong to the entire molecule.
- Electrons in **bonding MOs** stabilize the molecule; electrons in **antibonding MOs** destabilize it.

## Chapter 3
## Electrolytic Conductance

### 3.1 Fundamentals of Electrolysis

- **Electrolytes:** Substances that conduct electricity in the **molten or dissolved state** through ion migration.
- At the **anode** — oxidation occurs (loss of electrons).
- At the **cathode** — reduction occurs (gain of electrons).

**Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis:**

| Law | Statement |
|---|---|
| **First Law** | The mass ($m$) of a substance liberated at an electrode is proportional to the charge ($Q$) passed: $m = zIt$ |
| **Second Law** | For the same charge passed, the masses of different substances liberated are proportional to their **chemical equivalents** |

---

### 3.2 Conductance Parameters

| Parameter | Definition | Behaviour with Dilution |
|---|---|---|
| **Specific Conductance ($\kappa$)** | Conductance of a 1 cm³ solution | **Decreases** — fewer ions per unit volume |
| **Molar Conductance ($\Lambda_m$)** | Conductance of a solution containing 1 mole of electrolyte | **Increases** — greater ionic mobility and dissociation |
| **Cell Constant ($x$)** | Ratio of distance between electrodes to their area: $l/A$ | — |

---

### 3.3 Conductance Theories

#### Arrhenius Theory
- Electrolytes **dissociate into ions** in solution.
- The **degree of dissociation ($\alpha$)** increases with dilution.
- Applies well to **weak electrolytes**.

#### Debye-Hückel-Onsager Theory
- Explains conductance in **strong electrolytes** by accounting for two retarding effects:

| Effect | Description |
|---|---|
| **Relaxation (Asymmetry) Effect** | The ion atmosphere around a moving ion is asymmetric, creating a retarding force opposing ion motion |
| **Electrophoretic Effect** | The solvent molecules moving with the ion atmosphere oppose the motion of the central ion |

#### Kohlrausch's Law
At **infinite dilution**, the molar conductivity of an electrolyte equals the **sum of the conductivities of its individual ions**:

$$\Lambda_m^\infty = \lambda_+^\infty + \lambda_-^\infty$$

> 💡 This law is used to calculate the molar conductivity at infinite dilution for weak electrolytes, which cannot be measured directly by extrapolation.

## Chapter 4
## Electrochemical Cells

### 4.1 Cell Classification

| Cell Type | Energy Conversion | Nature of Reaction | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Electrolytic Cell** | Electrical energy → Chemical energy | **Non-spontaneous** (driven by external source) | Electrolysis of water |
| **Galvanic (Voltaic) Cell** | Chemical energy → Electrical energy | **Spontaneous** redox reaction | Daniel Cell ($Zn$/$Cu$) |

---

### 4.2 Electrode Potential and EMF

- **Electrode Potential:** The potential difference between an electrode and its ions in solution.
- **Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE):** The universal reference point for all electrode potentials, defined as **0.000 V**.

**Nernst Equation:**
Relates electrode potential to concentration and temperature:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF} \ln \frac{[\text{Products}]}{[\text{Reactants}]}$$

where:
- $E^\circ$ = standard electrode potential
- $R$ = gas constant
- $T$ = temperature in Kelvin
- $n$ = number of electrons transferred
- $F$ = Faraday's constant

**Free Energy and EMF:**

$$\Delta G = -nFE_{cell}$$

The maximum electrical work obtainable from a galvanic cell equals $-\Delta G$.

---

### 4.3 Specialized Cells and Concepts

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Concentration Cells** | Generate voltage based on **concentration differences** between identical electrodes in solutions of different concentrations |
| **Liquid Junction Potential** | A potential difference at the interface of two different electrolyte solutions; minimized by using a **salt bridge** |
| **Fuel Cells** | Galvanic cells that use a **continuous supply of reactants** (e.g., $H_2$ and $O_2$) to generate electricity without recharging |

## Chapter 5
## Applications of Conductance Measurements

### 5.1 Conductometric Titrations

- The **end-point** of a titration is detected by monitoring changes in **conductance** throughout the reaction.
- **Example — Strong Acid vs. Strong Base:**
  - Conductance **falls** as $H^+$ (high mobility) is replaced by the slower cation of the salt.
  - Reaches a **minimum at the neutralization point**.
  - Conductance **rises** again as excess base is added.

---

### 5.2 Solubility Determination

- Conductance measurements are used to determine the **solubility product ($K_{sp}$)** of sparingly soluble salts.
- **Examples:** $AgCl$, $BaSO_4$
- The specific conductance of a saturated solution is measured, and $K_{sp}$ is calculated using Kohlrausch's law values for the ions.

---

### 5.3 pH Measurement

- The **pH-dependent potential** of certain electrodes is used to measure solution acidity.
- **Electrodes used:**
  - **Glass Electrode** — most common; responds to $H^+$ concentration
  - **Quinhydrone Electrode** — an older method based on the redox potential of the quinhydrone system
- Both are coupled with a **reference electrode** to measure the potential difference and calculate pH.
